Escher's combinatorial patterns The experiments that Escher carried out from 1938 to 1942, producing scores of patterns by repeatedly sketching or printing a simple motif in a square grid according to a completely deterministic scheme was, for the artist, a pastime that eventually he tired of (see pages 44-52). Yet the combinatorial problems he set for himself as well as other problems suggested by his experiments have caught the eye of several mathematicians and computer scientists. Shortly after this book was published, the author received mail from two different computer scientists who wanted to determine exactly what Escher had done, and also to find, using a computer search, the answers to some of Escher's ques-tions. Escher had posed the following problem for himself: Choose an asymmetric motif, place it in a small square, then fill out a 2x2 grid of four squares with copies of this small decorated square in any orienta-tion. Translate the filled 2 × 2 grid horizontally and vertically to fill the plane. How many different patterns can be produced by a single motif, following this algorithm? Escher, by sheer doggedness (sketching all possibilities), correctly answered the question when only rotated orientations of the motif-filled square (chosen from four possible) are allowed: there are 23 different pat-terns. When both rotated and reflected versions (eight possible) of the motif-filled square are allowed, the problem is much more difficult, and Escher only examined two special cases: (1) in the 2 x 2 grid, two motifs have the same rotated orientation, and two motifs have the same reflected orientation, and (2) in the 2 x 2 grid, two motifs have different rotated ori-entations, and two motifs have different reflected orientations. In case (1), Escher found (again, through an exhaustive search by sketching) the correct answer: 10 different patterns. In case (2), it appears that he lost interest near the end of his search, yet he managed to find 36 different patterns. A computer program written by Eric Hanson found the answer to a more general version of Escher's two restricted cases: he allowed the 2 × 2 grid to contain any two rotated copies and any two reflected copies of the motif-filled square. For this case, he found there are 67 different patterns, (Schattschneider, M. C. Escher: Visions of Symmetry, pp 343–344)

In 1997, Dan Davis counted that there are \(154\) distinct tilings by \(2 \times 2\) stamps when both rotated and reflected versions are allowed.

Tilings on polyhedra

The number of tilings (\(X\)) of the polyhedron up to its symmetries (\(G\))
Take the average over all of the symmetries of the polyhedron
The number of tilings that look the same after \(g\) is applied

Let \(G\) be the group consisting of the \(12\) rotational symmetries of the tetrahedron.

How many ways can we decorate the faces the tetrahedron with these tile designs up to rotational symmetry?

How many ways are there to tile the tetrahedron using these four tile designs such that the tetrahedron looks the same after being rotated \(\pm120^\circ\) about a face (equivalently vertex)?
(There are \(8\) inequivalent ways in which we can do this.)
There is just \(1\) choice of tile for the rotating face.
Then we can choose any of the four tile designs for any of the remaining faces which then determines the other two.
Thus there are \(1 \times 4 = 4\) ways to tile the tetrahedron that look the same after \(120^\circ\) rotation of a face.
How many ways are there to tile the tetrahedron such that the tetrahedron looks the same after being rotated \(180^\circ\) along an edge?
(There are \(3\) inequivalent ways in which we can do this.)
We can choose a face and tile it any of the \(4\) ways.
This determines how we much tile the other face in its orbit.
Next we can choose either of the remaining faces and tile it any of the \(4\) ways, which determines the tiling on the fourth face.
Thus there are \(4 \times 4 = 16\) ways to tile the tetrahedron that look the same after \(180^\circ\) rotation.
How many ways are there to tile the tetrahedron such that the tetrahedron looks the same after doing nothing?
Everything looks the same after doing nothing!
We can tile each face \(4\) different ways.
Thus there are \(4^4 = 256\) ways to tile the tetrahedron that are fixed under the identity transformation.
Now applying Burnside's lemma we see that there are...
\[ \frac{3(16) + 8(4) + 1(256)}{12} = 28 \] distinct ways to tile the tetrahedron up to rotations.
My favorite shape is the pentagonal icositetrahedron. How many ways can tile the faces with \(3\)-colors up to its \(24\) symmetries?
A good guess is \[ \frac{3^{24}}{24} \approx 11\,767\,897\,353.4 \] using the heuristic "most colorings have no symmetry."
The correct answer is \[ A378475(3) = 11\,768\,099\,013, \] where A378475 is a sequence we added to the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS).

We start by describing matrix representatives for (generators of) the symmetry group of the polyhedron. For example, the full icosahedral group is a Coxeter group generated by the three reflections

\[ \begin{align*} R_0 &= \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\ R_1 &= \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 - \phi & -\phi & -1 \\ -\phi & 1 & 1-\phi \\ -1 & 1-\phi & \phi \end{pmatrix} \\ R_2 &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align*} \]

Next we give explicit vertex coordinates which we use to generate all of the vertices.

\[ \begin{align*} v &= (1,0,0) \\ v' &= \alpha(1,1,1) \\ v'' &= \beta(0,1,\phi) \end{align*} \] where \[ \begin{align*} \alpha &= (\sqrt 5 + 4)/11,\,\text{and} \\ \beta &= \phi/3. \end{align*} \]

With these vertices, we can explicitly compute how each \(g \in G\) acts on the faces and detect when and how "faces land on top of themselves" under repeated application of \(g \in G\). That is, we can compute when the size of an orbit of a face under the action of a cyclic subgroup \(\langle g \rangle\) is less than the order of \(g\).
When the size of the orbit is the order of the cyclic subgroup, we can freely choose our tile for that orbit. When it is smaller, we must choose an appropriately symmetric tile for that position, where the symmetry respects how the faces "land on top of themselves."
The number of ways of tiling the truncated icosahedron up to its \(60\) rotational symmetries with \(t_{5,\mathrm{id}}\) and \(t_{5,r}\) pentagonal tile designs fixed under the \(\mathrm{id}\), and \(r \in C_5\) respectively and \(t_{6,\mathrm{id}}\) and \(t_{6,r}\) hexagonal tile designs fixed under the \(\mathrm{id}\), and \(r \in C_6\) respectively is given by the expression \[ \begin{align*} &\frac{1}{60} \big( t_{6,\mathrm{id}}^{20} t_{5,\mathrm{id}}^{12} + 24t_{6,\mathrm{id}}^4 t_{5,\mathrm{id}}^2 t_{5,r}^2 \\ &\qquad~+ 20t_{6,\mathrm{id}}^6 t_{6,r}^2 t_{5,\mathrm{id}}^4 + 15t_{6,\mathrm{id}}^{10} t_{5,\mathrm{id}}^6 \big). \end{align*} \]
\[ \begin{alignat*}{2} t_{5,\mathrm{id}} &= 1 \qquad t_{5,r} &= 1 \\ t_{6,\mathrm{id}} &= 3 \qquad t_{6,r} &= 0 \end{alignat*} \]
There are \(8\,127\,868\) rotationally distinct "Truchet soccer balls".

We count "polyforms" (connected-components of polytopes) on a tiling, up to the symmetries of that tiling.

We begin by embedding the tiling in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), placing a vertex at the center of each cell, and edges between adjacent cells.

Connected components of cells on the tiling correspond to induced subgraphs on the corresponding adjacency graph.

Definition (sketch).
A polyomino is an orbit of induced subgraphs under a choice of subgroup of automorphism group of the graph.

How many \(1\)-celled polyforms?
How many \(2\)-celled polyforms?
How many \(3\)-celled polyforms?
How do we count the number of orbits of order-\(n\) induced subgraphs (polyforms)?
We need to generate representatives for every orbit.
We need to be able to tell if two representatives correspond to the same polyform.
This process is computationally expensive, so we want to encode this problem so that a computer can do the work for us.
We start by describing the Euclidean automorphisms, and a translation basis. \[\begin{align*} { \color{#FFA500} \vec{b}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ \sqrt3 \end{pmatrix} } \qquad { \color{#D555D5} \vec{b}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3\sqrt3 \end{pmatrix} } \end{align*}\] All vertices have rational coordinates with respect to this basis \(\mathcal{B} = \{\vec{b}_1, \vec{b}_2\}\).
The automorphism group is generated by \[ \left\langle \left[ \begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\\hline 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right], \left[ \begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 1\\\hline 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right], \left[ \begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 1 & 0\\ -1 & 0 & 0\\\hline 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right] \right\rangle, \] where the first two matrices generate the translation subgroup, and matrices act on projective coordinates.
Next we specify representatives for each of the orbits of the vertices in terms of the basis.
\[ (0,0) \quad (1/3,1/3) \quad (2/21,8/21) \]

Starting with the vertex orbit representatives, we can build all of the polyforms one cell at a time.
Next, we need to be able to determine when two polyforms are equivalent up to automorphisms. We do this by giving a canonical name for each orbit.
We start by shifting the polyform so that all coordinates are non-negative, and as small as possible.
Next, we move our piece into all possible orientations, which are specified by coset representatives of the quotient group of the Euclidean automorphism group by its translation subgroup.
