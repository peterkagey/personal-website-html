Human rhombic dodecahedron

by Peter Kagey (2025-11-27)

No, this isn't a human pyramid—it's a human rhombic dodecahedron!

Okay, technically this isn't a rhombic dodecahedron because of the quadrilateral at the top, but if the participants put their heads together (as shown in the animation below) then it has twelve rhombic faces. Close enough!

Some help from a LAPL librarian

I was curious about the provenance of this video. Who were these people? Is this a cultural practice somewhere in the world? Have they made other human polyhedra?

I had just finished up The Library Book by Susan Orlean, which intersperses stories about the 1986 fire at the LA Central Library with other stories of the LA Public Library system. In this book, I read about the helpfulness of LAPL librarians in finding information, so after unsuccessful sleuthing on my own, I decided to to reach out. Here's what I heard back:

I am a librarian in the Science & Technology Dept. at Central Library and I received your question. I was not able to find out much about the video itself. I used a tool I found online to download the video with hopes there would be some interesting metadata associated with the video, but I didn't unearth any meaningful clues. (Digital forensics is not my forte apparently) Poking around online, I did find out that in Spain there are "human tower" competitions and the human towers are called castells. It seems that competitors strive for tower height more than anything else. In India there is a festival, Dahi Handi, where "Govinda Pathak" teams make human towers in order to break a pot that is strung high up. So, once again the focus is on height over creativity.

Sincerely,
Stella Mittelbach
