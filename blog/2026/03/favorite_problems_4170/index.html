Students' favorite Abstract Algebra problems

by Peter Kagey (2026-03-13)

When I taught Introduction to Abstract Algebra I at Cal Poly Pomona in fall 2022, part of our homework assignments consisted of "choice exercises," where students had many options to choose from.

At the end of the term, I asked students to say what their favorite choice exercises from the term were, and I've included their answers here, with some slightly edited. The number of stars corresponds to the number of students who chose that exercise.

My personal favorite is probably HW 4.5! Click on the the problem numbers below to expand the text of the problem.

HW 2.13: Is this a group? (⭐️)

Choose any subset \(S\) of the following exercises such that \(|S| \leq 5\). (You get \(1\) point per exercise with a maximum of \(5\) points.)

  1. Prove or disprove that \((\{-1, 0, 1\}, \times)\) is a group.
  2. Prove or disprove that \((\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}, \times)\) is a group where \(\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}\) is the set of rational numbers with \(0\) removed.
  3. Prove or disprove that \((\{0\}, +)\) is a group.
  4. Prove or disprove that \((\{\dots, \frac{1}{16}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots\}, \times)\) is a group.
  5. Prove or disprove that if \(G\) and \(H\) are groups, then \(G \times H\) is a group under the operation \((g_1, h_1) \cdot (g_2, h_2) = (g_1 \cdot g_2, h_1 \cdot h_2)\).
  6. Prove or disprove that \(\{1, 5, 7, 11\}\) is a group under multiplication \((\text{mod } 12)\).
  7. Prove or disprove that the set of \(n \times n\) matrices with real valued entries forms a group under matrix multiplication.
  8. Prove or disprove that the set of \(n \times n\) matrices with real valued entries and a determinant of \(1\) forms a group under matrix multiplication.

HW 4.6: Non-equivalence relations (⭐️⭐️)

We begin with the definition of a relation. Given a set \(X\) we say a relation \(R\) is a subset of the Cartesian product \(R \subseteq X \times X\) and we say that \(a \mathrel{R} b\) if and only if \((a,b) \in R\).

For example, if \(X = \{1,2,3\}\) and \(R\) is the "strictly less than" relation, then formally, we write \[ R = \{(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)\}. \]

Do any subset of the following. You'll get \(1\) point if you find at least four, \(2\) points if you find at least six, \(3\) points if you find exactly seven, and \(4\) points if you find all eight.

  1. Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
  2. Reflexive, symmetric, and not transitive.
  3. Reflexive, not symmetric, and transitive.
  4. Reflexive, not symmetric, and not transitive.
  5. Not reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
  6. Not reflexive, symmetric, and not transitive.
  7. Not reflexive, not symmetric, and transitive.
  8. Not reflexive, not symmetric, and not transitive.
HW 4.7: Embedding a group (⭐️)

We can embed the dihedral group \(D_n\) into \(S_n\). (In fact, Cayley's theorem states that we can embed any finite group \(G\) into \(S_N\) for large enough some \(N\).) For example, if we mark the corners of the square \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), and \(4\), then rotating/reflecting the square permutes these numbers.

  1. Write down the eight permutations in \(S_4\) (in cycle notation) corresponding to the eight symmetries of the square.
  2. We can also write this in terms of the generators of a subgroup. Find two elements \(x, y \in S_4\) such that the eight permutations given above are equal to \(\langle x ,y \rangle\).
HW 4.10: A matrix representation (⭐️)

In Linear Algebra, we described a linear transformation using matrices. In particular, the linear transformation which sends the vector \((1,0) \mapsto (a_{11}, a_{21})\) and \((0,1) \mapsto (a_{12}, a_{22})\) is described by the matrix \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{bmatrix}. \]

  1. Check that this is true by multiplying the column vectors \(\vec{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) on the left by \(A\). (i.e. compute \(A\vec{e}_1\) and \(A\vec{e}_2\) and check that they match the above claim.)
  2. Use any tools you'd like to learn how to use TikZ to draw the square centered at the origin with vertices \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((-1,0)\), and \((0,-1)\).
  3. Give the matrix \(R\) that describes rotating by a positive \(90^\circ\).
  4. Give the matrix \(F\) that describes reflecting horizontally (over the \(y\)-axis).
  5. Check that \(\det(R) = 1\) and \(\det(F) = -1\). State in words what this means geometrically.
  6. Give the matrix \(X\) that describes rotating by a positive \(90^\circ\) followed by a horizontal reflection. Check that this is given by the matrix \(FR\), and state why we’re reading right-to-left.
HW 5.4: Homomorphisms and subgroups (⭐️⭐️⭐️)

Do any subset of any subset of the following:

  1. [≤4] For each part, give an example of two groups \(G_i\) and \(H_i\) and a homomorphism \({\varphi_i\colon G_i \to H_i}\) between them with the following properties.

    2. The homomorphism \(\varphi_2\colon G_2 \to H_2\) is neither 1-to-1 nor onto.
    3. The homomorphism \(\varphi_3\colon G_3 \to H_3\) is 1-to-1 but not onto.

HW 6.4: Test your intuition! (⭐️)

Attempt the following True/False questions and give explanations for your reasoning, then open the "True/False answers" at the end of the problem and check your answers. If you got the answer or explanation wrong, write the correct answer here and say something about why you got it wrong originally.

  1. Let \(G\) be a group with order \(n\), and denote the order of an element \(g \in G\) by \(|g|\). Then the order of \(G\) is equal to \[ \gcd(|g_1|, |g_2|, \dots, |g_n|) = n. \]
  2. If \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is a homomorphism that is 1-to-1, then \(\phi\colon G \to \operatorname{Im}(\phi)\) is a isomorphism.
  3. If \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is a homomorphism that is onto, then \(\operatorname{Im}(\phi) \triangleleft H\).
  4. If \(G\) is a non-abelian group and \(H\) is abelian, then the only homomorphism \(\phi \colon G \to H\) is the trivial homomorphism defined by \(\phi(g) = e_H\) for all \(g \in G\).
  5. If \(\varphi \colon G \to H\) is an isomorphism, then there always exists an isomorphism \({\psi \colon H \to G}\).
  6. Let \(G \times H\) be a direct product of groups. Then the set \[ N = \{(g, e_H) \mid g \in G\} \subseteq G \times H \] is a normal subgroup of \(G \times H\).
  7. If \(G/N \cong H\) then \(G \cong N \times H\).
  8. The map \(\varphi\colon \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z} \to C_{16}\) given by \(\varphi(1) = r \in C_{16}\) is a homomorphism.
  9. There is a homomorphism \(\psi\colon D_6 \to C_6\) such that \(\psi(r) = r \in C_6\) and \(\psi(f) = e \in C_6\).
  10. There is a homomorphism \(\phi\colon C_6 \to D_6\) such that \(\phi(r) = r \in D_6\).

True/False answers
  1. False. Consider \(G = C_2 \times C_2\).
  2. True. By definition, every map is (surjective) onto it's image, so this map is a bijective homomorphism.
  3. True. If it's onto, then \(\operatorname{Im}(\phi) = H\), and \(H \triangleleft H\).
  4. False. Let \(G = D_3\) and \(H = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\). Then the map which sends \(f \to 1\) and \(r \to 0\) is onto but not the trivial map.
  5. True. The inverse map \(\psi = \varphi^{-1}\) exists (because it's an bijection) and it is structure preserving. (See Exercise 7.1.)
  6. True. For all \(g_0 \in G\) and \(h \in H\), \((g_0, h) * (g, e_H) * (g_0, h)^{-1} = (g_0gg_0^{-1}, e_H) \in N\).
  7. False. Use the example above in part 4. The kernel is \(\langle r \rangle = \{e, r, r^2\}\), and the image is \(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\), but \(\langle r \rangle \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\) is abelian, so it's not isomorphic to \(D_3\).
  8. False. \(e = \varphi(0) = \varphi(4 + 4) \neq \varphi(4) * \varphi(4) = r^4 * r^4 = r^8\).
  9. False. Notice that \(rf = fr^5\), but \(\psi(rf) = \psi(r) * \psi(f) = r * e = r\), but \(\psi(fr^5) = \psi(f) * \psi(r)^5 = r^5\).
  10. True. \(C_6 = \langle r_c \mid r_c^6 \rangle\), and the map is structure preserving because \(\phi(r_c^6) = r_d^6 = e \in D_6\).

HW 6.8: Coin-flipping groups (⭐️)

Suppose that you have three (indistinguishable) coins, and you can flip over any subset of them and you can permute their places. Some examples of the states of these coins are HTT, THT, THT and so on. Let's call this group \(G\). We're going to show that this is related to the (Coxeter) group \(B_3\) of symmetries of the cube \[ B_3 = \langle r_1, r_2, r_3 \mid r_1^2 = r_2^2 = r_3^2 = (r_1r_2)^4 = (r_2r_3)^3 = (r_1r_3)^2 = e\rangle, \] where \(B_3\) consists of the \(48\) symmetries of the cube (including reflections).

  1. Show that \(G\) can be generated by the following three elements:
    1. \(x\), which is flipping over the left coin,
    2. \(s_1\), which is swapping the left and middle coins, and
    3. \(s_2\), which is swapping the middle and right coins.
  2. Show that there is a homomorphism \(\varphi\colon B_3 \to G\) with \(\varphi(r_1) = x\), \(\varphi(r_2) = s_1\), and \(\varphi(r_3) = s_2\).
  3. How many ways are there to choose a face of the cube, followed by an edge on that face, followed by a vertex on that edge? (This structure of \(v \subset e \subset f\) is called a "flag." And because the cube is "flag transitive," the number of flags is equal to the number of symmetries.)
  4. Prove that the homomorphism \(\varphi\colon B_3 \to G\) is an isomorphism.
  5. Here's the generalization to the symmetries of the \(n\)-dimensional hypercube: \[ B_n = \langle r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n \mid r_i^2 = (r_1r_2)^4 = (r_jr_{j+1})^3 = (r_kr_\ell)^2 = e, \text{ for } 1 < j < n \text{ and } |k - \ell| \geq 2 \rangle. \] In this case, we're dealing with \(n\) coins, and the homomorphism \(\varphi\colon G \to B_n\) sends
    • "flip over the leftmost coin" to \(r_1\) and
    • "swap coins \(k-1\) and \(k\)" to \(r_k\).
    Show that \(B_2\) is isomorphic to \(D_4\), the dihedral group of the square.
  6. Spend five minutes reading about or watching a YouTube video about "Coxeter groups," and say what you learned or are curious about.

HW 7.9: Adjacent transpositions (⭐️)

In the symmetric group \(S_n\), an adjacent transposition is a \(2\)-cycle where the indices differ by \(1\), which we write as \(s_i = (i\ i+1)\). For instance, \(s_2 = (2\ 3)\) or \(s_8 = (8\ 9)\) are adjacent transpositions, but \((1\ 9)\) is not. It turns out that every permutation in \(S_n\) can be written as a product of transpositions

  1. [1] Write the word \(524316\) as a product of adjacent transpositions. (Hint: what is the first letter of \(s_1 \circ s_2 \circ s_3 \circ s_4\) written as a word?)
  2. [1] Check this result in WolframAlpha by inputting permutation (1 2)(2 3) but with the transpositions found above. (Note that WolframAlpha reads the permutations left-to-right!)
  3. [3] If a permutation is written as a sequence of adjacent transpositions and there is no way to write it as a shorter sequence adjacent transpositions, then the sequence of adjacent transpositions is called a reduced word. (Note: a reduced word is a product of transpositions, not to be confused with writing a permutation in word notation.) We can move between reduced words by using the two identities:
    1. \(s_i \circ s_j = s_j \circ s_i\) if \(|i - j| > 2\)
    2. \(s_i \circ s_{i+1} \circ s_i = s_{i+1} \circ s_i \circ s_{i+1}\) for all \(1 \leq i < n - 1\).
    Prove these two identities, and use them to give an example of a reduced word that is different from the reduced word \( s_1 \circ s_3 \circ s_2 \circ s_3 \circ s_4 \circ s_3 \circ s_2 \circ s_1 \) but which is equal as a permutation.
  4. [5] Prove that every permutation in \(S_n\) can be written as a product of adjacent transpositions, and thus \[ \langle s_1, s_2, \dots, s_{n-1} \rangle = S_n. \]
  5. [3] While \(S_6\) cannot be generated by a single element, it can be generated by just two elements: \[ \langle (1\ 2)\underbrace{(1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6)}_c \rangle = S_6. \] Write the word \(524316\) in terms of \(s_1\) and \(c\).
